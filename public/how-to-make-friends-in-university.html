How to Make Friends in University

Starting university or entering a new academic year can feel surprisingly isolating, even in a crowded lecture theatre. Here are nine practical, low-pressure strategies to build lasting friendships, find study partners, and connect with people on your campus.

The Campus Loneliness Paradox: You Are Not the Only One

One of the best-kept secrets of higher education is that almost everyone feels lonely at some point during their first few semesters. You walk into massive lecture halls filled with hundreds of students, walk past crowded cafeterias, and assume everyone else arrived with a pre-packaged social circle. In reality, the vast majority of students are nervous, unsure of their footing, and quietly waiting for someone else to initiate conversation.

Friendship in university does not happen by accident; it happens through repeated, unplanned interactions combined with shared vulnerability. By taking small, deliberate steps each day, you can transform campus from an intimidating sea of strangers into a warm, supportive community.

Strategies 1 & 2: Orientation Week and Lecture Hall Proximity

1. Take Full Advantage of Orientation Week (O-Week): O-Week is the one time when nobody knows anyone and social barriers are completely down. Attend icebreakers, campus tours, departmental welcomes, and society showcases. Even if you feel shy, simply standing near groups and introducing yourself with your name, faculty, and hometown is 100% normal and welcomed.

2. Practice the "Three-Seat Rule" in Lectures: In large lecture theatres, don't sit in the back corner surrounded by empty chairs. Sit within three seats of someone. Arrive five minutes early and ask an easy, situational question: "Hey, is this seat taken?", "Did you manage to get through the prep reading for this week?", or "Do you know which textbook chapter we are on today?" These low-stakes interactions break the ice naturally.

Strategies 3 & 4: Study Groups and Campus Societies

3. Form or Join Course Study Groups: Academic collaboration is one of the most effective ways to build deep, enduring friendships. When a major assignment, test, or tutorial is announced, turn to the person next to you and say: "A few of us are comparing tutorial questions after class—do you want to join the WhatsApp study group?" Study sessions naturally lead to sharing snacks, venting about exam stress, and grabbing lunch together.

4. Join at Least One Interest Society and One Professional Club: Student societies provide instant common ground. Join one club purely for fun (e.g. photography, hiking, gaming, debate, or drama) and one related to your field of study (e.g. Law Students' Association, Engineering Society, or Business League). Attend the weekly meetings consistently; seeing familiar faces week after week creates natural bonds without awkward small talk.

Strategies 5 & 6: Residence Life and Everyday Campus Hangouts

5. Maximize Residence and Hostel Common Areas: If you live on campus or in private student accommodation, resist the urge to lock yourself in your room 24/7. Keep your door propped open when unpacking or studying casually during the first two weeks. Spend time in communal kitchens, TV lounges, and laundry rooms. Offering to share snacks or asking how someone's day went turns neighbours into close friends.

6. Establish Consistent "Third Spaces" on Campus: Humans build familiarity through repeated exposure. Pick two or three spots on campus—a specific floor of the library, an outdoor courtyard bench, or a campus cafe—and study there regularly. When fellow students notice you consistently, saying hello becomes second nature.

Strategies 7, 8 & 9: Digital Discovery, Following Up, and Quality Over Quantity

7. Use Student Social Apps with a "Friends" Intention: Platforms like The College Date are not just for romance. By setting your intention to "Find Friends," you can discover coursemates and campus peers who share your interests, music taste, and study goals, providing a pressure-free digital bridge to real-world campus meetups.

8. Follow Up Within 48 Hours: When you connect with someone promising, don't let the momentum fade. Send a quick, low-pressure message within a couple of days: "Hey, great chatting after Economics on Tuesday! Let me know if you want to grab coffee before Friday's tutorial." Friendship requires initiative.

9. Focus on Quality Over Quantity: You don't need a hundred campus acquaintances to thrive at university. Having two or three reliable, empathetic friends who check in on you, study with you, and have your back will make your university experience rich, fulfilling, and grounded.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I make friends at university if I am an introvert?

Focus on small, structured group settings like laboratory practicals, departmental study groups, or niche campus clubs where conversation centers around a shared activity rather than open-ended mingling.

Is it too late to make friends if I missed Orientation Week?

Not at all. Most lasting university friendships form throughout the semester in tutorials, study groups, campus societies, and residence dining halls long after O-Week has concluded.

Can I use The College Date just to find friends and study buddies?

Yes. The College Date allows you to specify "Find Friends" as your primary intention, helping you match with students who are looking for platonic social connections and study partners.

What should I do if a friendship feels one-sided?

If you are always the one reaching out and making plans without reciprocal effort, take a step back and invest your energy in other peers who show genuine enthusiasm to spend time with you.
